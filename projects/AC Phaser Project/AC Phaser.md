## AC Fundamentals

**Alternating Current** (AC) is electrical current where the flow of electrons periodically reverses direction and changes in magnitude, following a wave-like pattern (sine wave). It is the standard format used for the long-distance transmission, distribution, and consuption of electricity in homes and businesses worldwide.

**Fundamentals of AC**:

- Waveform: AC voltage and current alternate continuously. The standard AC wave is a sinusoidal curve that goes through positive and negative values.

- Frequency ($f$): The number of complete cycles the wave completes in one second; measured in *Hertz* (Hz). The grid power in the U.S. operates at 60Hz.

- Amplitude & Peak Value: The maximum value (voltage or current) a sine wave reaches from its zero baseline.

- RMS (Root Mean Square) Value: Bacause an AC wave is constanly changing, standard power is often expressed as an RMS value. RMS is the equivalent DC value that would deliver the exact same amount of heating or power to a resistor. 

- Phase ($\phi$): The position of an AC waveform relative to a specific reference point or another waveform. In AC circuits, voltage and current are often "out of phase" with each other, meaning their peaks do not align perfectly in time.

- Impedance ($Z$): The AC equivalent of resistance. While DC only face resistance from wires and componenets, AC also faces opposition from capacitance and inductance.

- Power Factor: A measure of how effectively electrical power is being used in an AC circuit. It is the ratio of real power (used to do work) to apparent power (total power flowing in the circuit).

## Waveforms

A *sinusoidal waveform* is a mathematical curve that describes a smooth, repetitive oscillation. In AC systems, voltage and current take this shape because it represents the natural kinetic motion of a wire coil rotating at a constant speed inside a uniform magnetic field.

Mathematically, a standard AC voltage wave is expressed over time ($t$) as:

$$v(t) = V_msin(\omega t + \phi)$$

Where:
- Amplitude ($V_m$ or $I_m$): is the maximum peak value of the wave measured from its center zero-line. It represents the highest voltage or current magnitude the signal reaches in either the positive or negative direction.
- Period ($T$): is the time taken for one complete cycle of the wave to repeat itself. It spans one full positive alternation and one full negative alternation. It is measured in seconds ($s$).
- Frequency ($f$): is the number of complete cycles per second. It is the direct inverse of the period ($f = {1 \over T}$) and is measured in Hertz(Hz). 
-Angular Frequency ($\omega$): represents the rotational rate of the wave in radians per second (rad/s). Becuse one full cycle equals $2\pi$ radians, it is calculated using the formula:

$$\omega = 2 \pi f = {2 \pi \over T}$$

## Root Mean Square (RMS)

Root Mean Square (RMS) value is used for AC power calculations because it represents the "heating quivalent" of a DC power source. If an AC current with an RMS value of 2 Amps passes through a resistor, it generates the exact same amount of thermal heat as 2 Amps of steady DC current.

Calculating the average value of a standard sinusoidal voltage or current over a full cycle results in a value of zero. The positive and negative halves of the wave cacel each other out. Power, $P = I^2 \times R$ or $P = {V^2 \over R}$, is not zero. The current or voltage is squared, so any negative values become positive. Therefore, power is always positive.

The term "Root Mean Square" is literally the step-by-step mathematical recipe:

1. Square: The entire sinusoidal wave is squared. 
2. Mean: The athematical average (mean) of the squared wave is calculated over one full period.
3. Root: The square root of the mean value is taken to bring the unit scale back down from volts-squared or amps-squared to normal Volts or Amps.

For a perfect sine wave:

$$V_{rms} = {V_{peak} \over \sqrt {2}} \approx 0.707 \times V_{peak}$$

Conversely, the $V_{peak}$ from a known RMS voltage is:

$$V_{peak} = V_{rms} \times \sqrt 2 \approx 1.414 \times V_{rms}$$

**Note:** non-sinusoidal waveforms and complex combinations of waves are highly expected and dominate modern power grids. While *voltage* delivered by utility companies remains a relatively clean sine wave, the *current* pulled by modern appliances is almost always non-sinusoidal. The standard $0.707 \times V_{peak}$ rule fails completely when waveforms distort. **Crest factor**, the ratio of peak value to RMS value, is the metric used to track how far a wave has drifted from a perfect sinusoid. 

Common non-sinusoidal waveforms:
- Square Waves (Crest factor = 1.0): The signal instantly jumps to its peak, stays there, and drops. Because it spending all its time at the peak value, $V_{rms} = V_{peak}$. These are generated by basic uninterruptible power supplies (UPS) or cheap car inverters.
- Triangle/Sawtooth Waves (Crest facro = 1.732): The voltage rises and falls in straight linear ramps. Its RMS formula is $V_{rms} = {V_{peak} \over \sqrt 3} \approx V_{peak}$. These are commonly found in audio equipment, synthesizers, and television scanning circuits.
- Pulsed/Chopped Waves (High Crest Factors): Modern computers, LED lights, and battery chargers use Switch-Mode Power Supplies (SMPS). Instead of pulling current smoothly, they "grab" power in short, abrupt spikes only at the absolute peak of the voltage cycle. This creates a highly jagged, spiky wave where the crest factor can shoot up to 3.0 or 5.0.

Whenever a device draws current in a non-sinusoidal shape, it introduces harmonic distortion. According to a mathematical principle known as Fourier Analysis, any distorted, repeating waveform is actually the sum of a fundamental sine wave and a series of smaller sine waves at integer multiples of that frequency, known as harmonics. When multiple harmoic waves combine, calculating the true total RMS value requires a geometric sum of a ll the individual waveforms:

$$V_{rms(total)} = \sqrt {V_1^2 + V_2^2 + V_3^2 + ... + V_n^2}$$

Where $V_1$ is the RMS of the fundamental frequency and $V_2$, $V_3$ are the RMS values of the harmonics.

## Phase Shift

Physically, a phase shift represents a timing delay between two waves. Even though the voltage and current waves are cycling at the exact same frequency, the do not hit their peaks, zeroes, or minimums at the same microsecond.

### Why Current Lags Voltage in an Inductor (ELI)

An inductor is a coil of wire. When AC current is pushed through a coil, it creates an expanding and collapsing magnetic field. This magnetic field physically fights back against any change in current.

According to Faraday's and Lenz's Laws, changing current creates a changing magnetic field, which induces a "back-EMF" (a counter-voltage) that opposes the incoming voltage. When AC voltage across an inductor is first applied, the current cannot instantly jump up because the magnetic field is actively throttling it. The result is the voltage wave peaks first while the current is still struggling to fight through the magnetic inertia. Consequently, the **E**lectromotive force (Voltage) leads the **I**ntensity (current) in an **L**nductor (The L comes from Heinrich Lenz). In a purely inductive circuit, current lags exacly 90° behind voltage.

### Why Current Leads Voltage in a Capacitor (ICE)

A capacitor consists of two parallel metal plates separated by an insulator. It does not let electrons pass through it; instead it stores them as an electrostatic field.

When a capacitor is a zero charge/Voltage, current is at its peak and electrons rush into the capacitor at maximum speed. As the negative plate fills with electrons, they repel electrons from the positive plate. The build-up of static charge creates a voltage across the capacitor plates that fights the supply line. The consequence is the current hits its absolute peak at the very beginning of the cycle when the capacitor is empty and easy to fill. The voltage only reaches its peak later once the plates are completely packed with charge and the current drops to zero. Therefor, **I**ntensity (current) leads **C**apacitance voltage (ICE). In a purely capacitive circuit, current leads voltage by exactly 90 degrees.

**Mechanical Analogy**:
- Inductors act like flywheel masses. Pushing a flywheel applies a force (Voltage) instantly, but it takes time fore the wheel's rotation speed (Current) to catch up.
- Capacitors act like springs. Compressing a spring applies motion (Current) instantly. Counter-force (Voltage) builds up once the spring is compressed.

## Reactive Components

### Inductive Reactance

**Inductive Reactance ($X_L$):** the opposition that an inductor offers to alternating current (AC), measured in Ohms ($\Omega$). Unlike a simple resistor, which resists current by converting electrical energy into physical heat, an inductor opposes current by storing energy in its magnetic field and releasing it back into the circuit.

Inductive reactance scales directly and linearly with frequency. As the frequency of the AC signal increases, the inductive reactance increases proportionally. The formula that describes this relationship is:

$$X_L = 2 \pi fL = \omega L$$

- $X_L$ = Inductive Reactance in Ohms ($\Omega$).
- $f$ = Frequency in Hertz (Hz).
- $L$ = Inductance in Henries (H).
- $\omega$ = Angular frequency in radians per second (rad/s).

**The Physical Reason for this Scaling**

- A low Frequencies: The voltage and current change very slowly. Because the magnetic field expands and collapses slowly, the induced counter-voltage (back-EMF) is incredibly weak. At a frequency of 0Hz (Direct Current), the reactance drops to zero, and the inductor acts like a simple short circuit with a stable magnetic field.
- At high frequencies: The current tries to change direction millions of times per second. Because the current changes so rapidly, the magnetic field is forced to expand and collapse at a violent pace. This creates a missive back-EMF that fiercely fights the incoming current. At extremely high frequencies, the reactance becomes so large that the inductor acts like an open circuit.

**Application: Low-Pass Filters**

Because of this specific scaling property, inductors are used to build low-pass filters. When a mixed signal (containing both high-frequency and low-frequency data) passes through an inductor, the low frequencies glide right through with minimal opposition, while the high-frequency noise is completely blocked by the masive reactance.

### Capacitive Reactance

**Capacitive reactance ($X_C$)** is the opposition that a capacitor offers to alternating current (AC), measured in Ohms ($\Omega$). Like an inductor, it opposes current without wasting energy as heat. Instead it blocks current by storing energy inside its internal electrostatic field.

Unlike an inductor, capacitive reactance scales inversely with frequency. As the frequency of the AC signal increases, the opposition offered by the capacitor drops sharply. The formula for this relationship is:

$$X_C = {1 \over {2 \pi f C}} = { 1 \over {\omega C}}$$

where:
- $X_C$ = Capacitive reactance in Ohms ($\Omega$).
- $f$ = Frequency in Hertz (Hz).
- $C$ = Capacitance in Farads (F).
- $\omega$ = Angular frequency in radians per second (rad/s)

**The Physical Reason for this Scaling**

- At 0Hz (Direct Current): Electrons rush onto the capacitor plates until they are completely full. Once full charged, the capacitor's internal voltage perfectly matches the supply voltage, effectively acting like an open circuit (infinite resistance).
- At Low Frequencies: The voltage changes slowly. The capacitor has plenty of time to fill up with electrons during each long half-cycle. As the plates get saturated, they create high backpressure that heavily resists any further electron flow. Reactance is high.
- At High Frequencies: The voltage changes direction millions of times per second. Because the cycles are incredibly short, the capacitor barely has time to deposit a few electrons onto its plates before the voltage flips and pulls them right back off. Since the plates never get full, they never build up a strong counter-voltage to fight the incoming current. Electrons rush back and forth with total freedom. At extremely high frequencies, the capacitor acts like a short circuit.

**Applications: High-Pass Filters**

Because capacitive reactance drops at high frequencies, engineers use capacitors to create high-pass filters. In audio equipment, a capacitor is placed in line with a tweeter speaker. It presents a massive reactance to deep, low-frequency bass notes (blocking them from blowing out the speaker), while letting high-frequency treble notes pass through with virtually no opposition.

## Net Reactance

Net reactance ($X$) is the total combined opposition to alternating current resulting from the opposing behaviors of indictors and capacitors in a circuit. Because inductors and capacitors shift current and voltage in completely opposite directions, their reactances actively fight and cancel each other out. Mathematically, net reactance is calculated by subtracting capacitive reactance from inductive reactance:

$$X = X_L - X_C$$

### What It Means When Net Reactance Goes Negative

When net reactance is negative ($X < 0$), it means that capacitive reactance is greater than inductive reactance ($X_C > X_L$). Physically and behaviorally, a negative net reactance tells you three critical things about the circuit:

- The circuit is capacitive: The circuit behaves predominantly like a capacitor. Even though inductors may be present, their ability to store energy in magnetic fields is entirely overwhelmed by the capacitors storing energy in electrostatic fields.
- Current Leads Voltage: Because the circuit is overall capacitive, it follows the ICE rule. The total current wave will peak before the total voltage wave in time. The exact phase angle ($\phi$) between the total voltage and current will be negative.
- Reactance decreases as frequency rises: If you increase the frequency of the AC supply while the net reactance is negative, the value of $X_C$ will shrink while $X_L$ grows. This causes the net reactance to move closer to zero, make the circuit less capacitive.

### Net Reactance on the Complex Plane

In electrical engineering, reactances are plotted on a 2D grid called a phasor diagram or complex plane:

- Inductive reactance ($X_L$) is plotted pointing straight up on the imaginary axis ($+j$).
- Capacitive reactanve ($X_C$) is plotted pointing straight down on the imaginary axis ($-j$)

                +j (Inductive Reactance X_L)
                    ^

        ------------+------------> +R (Real Resistance)

When you calculate $X_L - X_C$, the two vectors pull against eachother. If the downward $X_C$ vector is longer, the resulting net reactance vector points down into the negative territory.

**The Three States of Net Reactance**

| Net Reactance Value | Circuit Type | Wave Behavior | Frequency Relationship |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Positive ($X > 0$) | Inductive ($X_L > X_C$) | Current lags voltage (ELI) | Raising frequency increases opposition. |
| Zero ($X = 0$) | Resonant ($X_L = X_C$) | Current and voltage are pefectly in phase | The reactances completely cancel out. | 
|Negative ($X < 0$) | Capacitive ($X_C > X_L$) | Current leads voltage (ICE) | Raising frequency decreases opposition. |

Viewing the phasor diagram, the combination of impedance and resistance can be expressed in the form:

${Z = R + jX}$

Where:
- R is the real component; normal resistive elements in a circuit.
- X is the complex component; Ractive elements in the circuit. +j for more inductive and -j for more capacitive.

As a result, voltage and current will only be out of phase by exactly 90 degrees if the circuit contains zero real resistance ($R = 0$). As long as there is real resistance in the circuit alongside a non-zero net reactance, the pahse angle will fall somewhere between 0 and 90 degrees.

The exact phase shift angle ($\phi$) is governed by the impedance triangle and calculated using trigonometry:

$$\phi = \arctan ({X \over R})$$

The phase shift angle changes based on which component dominates the circuit:

- Pure reactance ($R = 0, X \neq 0$): If a circuit could exist with absolutely no resistance, the formula becomes $\arctan ({X \over 0})$, which yields exactly $\pm90^\circ$.
- Highly Reactive ($X \gg R$): If the reactance is massive compared to a tiny resistance, the phase angle will approach $90^\circ$.
- Highly Resistive ($R \gg X$): If the real resistance is massive compared to the reactance, the phase angle collapses down close to zero.
- Equal Parts ($R = X$): The the resistance and net reactance have the exact same value in ohms, the formula is $\arctan(1)$, meaning the current and voltage will be out of phase by exactly $45^\circ$.

As a note, $\left| Z \right| = \sqrt {R^2 + X^2}$ describes the magnitude of impedance, but loses the phase direction. A single scalar is inadequate for describing whether the circuit is inductive or capacitive. The complex form $R + jX$ perserves both magnitude and sign (direction simultaneously).

## Power Factor

Power factor (PF) is the ratio of actual power used to do work in a circuit to the total power supplied to the circuit. It measures how efficiently a electrical system converts incoming electrical current into useful work output, such as heat, light, or mechanical rotation. It is expressed as a number between 0 and 1 (or 0% to 100%):

$$ \text {Power Factor} = {\text {Real Power (Watts, W)} \over \text {Apparent Power (Volt-Amps, VA)}} = \cos(\phi)$$

### The Three Types of AC Power

To understand power factor, visualize power split into three distinct categories on a vector triangle:

1. Real Power ($P$): Measured in Watts (W). This is the actual energy performing real physical work, like heating a coil, spinning a conveyor belt, or lighting an LED bulb.
2. Reactive Power ($Q$): Measured in Volt-Amps Reactive (VAR). This power does zero useful work. It is the energy that constantly bounces back and forth between the power source and the magnetic or electrostatic fields of inductors and capacitors.
3. Apparent Power ($S$): Measured in Volt-Amps (VA). This is the total geometric combination of Real and Reactive power. It represents the total amount of raw electricity the power grid must generate and transmit down the wires to run equipment.

                  /|
                 / |
        Apparent/  | Reactive Power (Q)
        Power  /   | (Bounces back and forth)
         (S)  /    |
             /_____|
              Angle (φ) 
             Real Power (P) (Does actual work)

If the phase angle ($\phi$) between the voltage and current waves increases due to large reactances, the vertical leg of the triangle (Reactive Power) grows. This forces the hypotenuse (Apparent Power) to grow as well, lowering the efficiency of the system.

### What "Unity Power Factor" Means for Load

Unity Power Factor (or electrical resonance) means the power factor is exactly 1.0 (or 100%). When an electrical load achieves unity power factor, it represents a state of perfect electrical efficiency:

- Zero Reactive Power ($Q = 0$): The load does not absorb or stor temporary energy in magnetic or electrostatic fields. Apparent Power equals Real Power ($S = P$), meaning every single volt-amp delivered by the grid is converted directly into useful work.
- Voltage and Current are in phase: The phase angle ($\phi$) is exactly 0 degrees. Because $\cos (0^\circ) = 1$, the voltage wave and the current wave cross the zero-line and hit their peaks at the exact same microsecond.
- The load is ruely resistive: The net reactance of the circuit is zero ($X = 0$). This happens if the load is naturally a simple resistor (like an electric heater or oven) or if engineers have added external capacitors to perfectly cancel out the inductive reactance of industrial motors (a process called Power Factor Correction).

Mathematically:

$X_L = \omega L$ and $X_C = {1 \over \omega C}$

Unity Power Factor happens when $X_L = X_C$.

$\omega L = {1 \over \omega C}$

$\omega ^ 2 = {1 \over LC} \implies \omega_0 = {1 \over \sqrt {LC}}$

**Why Unity Power Factor is the Ultimate Goal**

If a large factory has a poor power factor (e.g., 0.70), it means they are drawing massive amounts of extra current just to magnetize their motors, forcing the utility grid to provide heavy cables and massive transformers to support them, even thought that extra current doesn't register on a standard wattmeter.

By Correcting the system to Unity Power Factor, the required current drops to the absolute minimum required to run the machines. This stops wires from overheating, prevents voltage drops, and eliminates costly penalty fees applied by power companies.
