-------Ohm's Law--------
────── ⋆⋅☆⋅⋆ ────────

Ohm's law states that the electric current through
a conductor between two points is directly proportional
to the voltage across the two points.

V = IR; I = V/R; R = V/I

Where I is the current (Amps) through the conductor, V is the
voltage (Volts) measured *across* the conductor and R is the
resistance (Ohms) of the conductor.

If the resistance is not constant, the equation cannot
be called Ohm's law. Materials that do not obey Ohm's law
are called non-ohmic. Examples of non-constant resistance
include diodes and filament lamps. Resistance can
change based on factors like temperature, voltage,
and current levels

----Power Dissipation----
────── ⋆⋅☆⋅⋆ ────────

Power dissipation refers to the process where electrical
energy is converted into hear within an electronic
component or circuit. This phenomenon occurs due to the
resistance encountered by the electric current as it flows
through materials, leading to energy loss that manifests
as heat.

===Types of Power Dissipation===

+ Dynamic Power Dissipation
  - Occurs during circuit operation when the circuit is active
  - Primarily caused by the charging and discharging of capacitors
within the circuit
  - Proportional to the square of the voltage and the frequency
of the operation

+ Static Power Dissipation
  - Happens when the circuit is not actively switching but
still consumes power.
  - Mainly due to leakage currents in transistors when they
are in an off state.

P = IV; P = I²R; P = V²/R

Where current I flows through a given element, losing voltage
V in the process. The power dissipated is P (Watts).

=== Resistor Power Ratings ===

Resistors have power ratings to indicate the maximum amount of
electrical power they can safely dissipate as heat without
being damages.

+ Prevents Overheating
  - Resistors convert electrical energy in to heat when 
current flows through them. If the power dissipated exceeds
the resistor's rating, it can overheat, leading to failure
or changes in resistance.

+ Ensuring Reliability
  - Proper pwer ratings help maintain the reliability of 
electronic circuits. Using a resistor with an appropriate power
rating prevents circuit failure and safety hazards.
 
+ Design Considerations
  - Engineers must consider the power rating when designing
circuits to ensure components operate within safe limits.
Selecting resistors with higher power ratings than required 
provides a safety margin.

-----Voltage Divider-----
────── ⋆⋅☆⋅⋆ ────────

A voltage divider is a passive linear circuit that produces 
an output voltage (V_out) that is a fraction of its input
voltage (V_in). Voltage division is the result of *distributing*
the input voltage among the components of the divider. A
simple example of a voltage divider is two resistors connected
in series, with the input voltage applied across the resistor pair
and the output voltage emerging from the connection between them.

A voltage divider referenced to ground is created by connecting
two electrical impedances (resistors in this case) in series. 
The input voltage is applied across the series impedances R1 and R2 
and the output is the voltage across R2. (Impedances can be 
composed of other elements such as inductors and capacitors).

Vin		If the current in the output is zero the the
+		relationship between the input voltage, V_in
|		and the output voltage, V_out is:
R1		
|		V_out = V_in * (R2 / (R1 + R2))
+---- Vout
|		Voltage is distributed proportionally to 
R2		components. If R1 = R2, then
|		V_out = 1/2 * Vin.
-
GND		Any ratio V_out / V_in greater than 1 is not 
		possible. Using resistors alone, it is not
possible to either invert the voltage or increase V_out above V_in.

Solving for an unknown resistor is done using algebra:

R1 = R2 * (V_in / V_out - 1)

R2 = R1 * (1 / (V_in / V_out - 1))

------Potentiometer------
────── ⋆⋅☆⋅⋆ ────────

A potentiometer is a three-terminal resistor with a sliding
or rotating contact that forms an adjustable voltage divider.
In motion control systems, potentiometers are frequently used
as position sensors to provide analog feedback to a controller,
allowing for precise tracking of mechanical movement

End A      Resistive track        End B
o-----[======================]-----o
\              ^
\              |
\              | Wiper (middle terminal)
\              |
o--------------┘

Potentiometers are essentially voltage dividers. Common uses
are electronic control devices such as volume controls or 
speed controls in fans. Potentiometers are rarely used to
directly control significant power (more than 1 watt), since
power dissipated in the potentiometer would be comparable to
the power in the controlled load.

Using only two terminals (one end and the wiper), makes the
pot act as a variable resistor or rheostat.

--Resistor Color Codes--
────── ⋆⋅☆⋅⋆ ────────

The value of resistance offered by a resistor is marked using
a color code. Small printing of tiny elements is not feasible.
Thus, the value of the resistor is displayed using color bands. 

|   |   |   |	   |   |   |   |   |
B1  B2  M   T  or  B1  B2  B3  M   T
│   │  │   │      |   |   |   |   |

B1, B2, B3 are digits
M is the multiplier (10^n)
T gives the tolerance

The tolerance band (gold/silver) is always on the right.

------Arduino ADC--------
────── ⋆⋅☆⋅⋆ ────────

Arduino boards use Analog-to-Digital Converters (ADCs) to
convert signals from sensors into digital values that can
be processed by the microcontroller. This is essential for
applications where sensors output analog signals, such as
temperature or pressure readings.

The Arduino Mega has a resolution of 10 bits or 1024 
distinct values (0-1023).

To convert the ADC reading to a corresponding voltage, the 
conversion formula is used:

V_out = (D_out * V_max) / D_max

Where V_out = output voltage
      D_out = raw ADC digital reading
      V_max = Maximum measurable input voltage (5V for the Mega)
      D_max = Maximum ADC raw digital reading (1023 for 10-bits)

ex: For a ADC reading of 512,
    V_out = (512 * 5) / 1023 = ~2.5V

=== Sampling Rate ===

When the sampling rate is increased, the ADC can capture
more data points over time. This allows for a more accurate
representation of fast-changing signals, which can
enhance the effective resolution. Conversely, if the sampling
rate is too low, it may not adequately capture the nuances
of a rapidly changing signal, leading to potential data loss
or aliasing.

According to the Nyquist theorem, the sampling rate must be
at least twice the highest frequency of the input signal to
avoid aliasing.
